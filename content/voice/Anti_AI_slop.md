No word or phrase can prove a text was AI-generated. The most defensible approach is to look for several signals clustering together, compare them with the author’s prior work, and treat automated-detector scores as leads—not verdicts. OpenAI explicitly says reliable detection of all AI-written text is impossible, and Turnitin withholds detailed scores below 20% because this range is more prone to false positives. [OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/new-ai-classifier-for-indicating-ai-written-text/), [Turnitin](https://guides.turnitin.com/hc/en-us/articles/22774058814093-Using-the-AI-Writing-Report)

## **Common AI-like phrases and templates**

These are useful as pattern clues when several occur in a short passage.

**Generic opening and framing**

* “In today’s rapidly evolving landscape…”  
* “In the realm of…”  
* “At its core…”  
* “This article/post explores…”  
* “Let’s delve into…”  
* “It is important to note that…”  
* “It is worth noting that…”  
* “A key aspect to consider is…”  
* “This underscores the importance of…”  
* “This highlights the need for…”

* “Moreover,”  
* “Furthermore,”

* “In addition,”  
* “That said,”  
* “With that in mind,”  
* “As a result,”  
* “Consequently,”  
* “Ultimately,”

Research comparing prompted GPT writing with human writing found GPT used additive transitions—especially “moreover,” “furthermore,” “also,” and “in addition”—more often. [Modern Language Journal study](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/modl.70089)

## **Structural signals that matter more than a word list**

Look for a cluster of these:

* Uniformly polished grammar, sentence length, and paragraph rhythm.  
* A predictable essay arc: broad introduction, three tidy points, generic conclusion.  
* Repeated transitions at the start of nearly every paragraph.  
* Many abstract claims but few concrete, verifiable details, lived experiences, or source-specific observations.  
* Symmetrical “pros and cons” treatment even where a real author would likely take a clear position.  
* Excessive restatement of the prompt or conclusion.  
* Safe, agreeable tone; weak authorial stance; few genuine uncertainties.  
* Definitions and explanations that are technically fluent but shallow or noncommittal.  
* Generic examples that could fit almost any topic.  
* Overuse of headings, bullets, “Key takeaways,” FAQs, or explanatory asides when the requested format did not call for them.  
* Frequent em dashes, parenthetical qualifiers, and long appositive phrases—again, only as supporting evidence.

Stylometry research finds that detection works best from combinations of lexical, grammatical, syntactic, punctuation, and distributional features—not individual “AI words.” [Stylometry study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0957417425026181)

## **A practical evidence scale**

| Signal | How to interpret it |
| ----- | ----- |
| One “AI word,” e.g. *delve* | No meaningful evidence |
| Several watchwords in a short passage | Weak clue |
| Watchwords \+ formulaic transitions \+ generic claims | Moderate reason to inspect |
| Pattern cluster \+ sharp shift from the author’s established voice | Stronger concern |
| Pattern cluster \+ no drafts, sources, notes, or ability to explain choices | Basis for a conversation, not automatic punishment |

A careful reviewer should preserve drafts and revision history, ask the author to explain sources and choices, compare with known writing, and verify claims/citations. Do not accuse someone based on vocabulary alone—these words are increasingly entering ordinary human writing, which makes lexical “tells” less reliable over time. [ACL coevolution research](https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-acl.657/)

Write in a clear, natural, human voice. Prioritize specificity, honest judgment, and useful detail over polished-sounding generalities.

Avoid AI-associated filler and elevated vocabulary unless it is genuinely the most precise word. In particular, avoid or use sparingly: “delve,” “tapestry,” “landscape,” “realm,” “intricate,” “pivotal,” “crucial,” “underscore,” “foster,” “showcase,” “vibrant,” “testament,” “meticulous,” “seamless,” “multifaceted,” “transformative,” “leverage,” “navigate,” and “unlock.”

Avoid stock phrases and replace them with direct statements:

* Do not write “In today’s rapidly evolving landscape.” Start with the actual subject.  
* Do not write “It is important to note.” State the important point.  
* Do not write “This underscores/highlights the importance of.” Explain the consequence.  
* Do not write “plays a crucial role.” Say what it does.  
* Do not write “has the potential to.” State the likely outcome and its limits.  
* Do not write “a wide range of benefits.” Name the two or three actual benefits.  
* Do not write “in the realm of.” Use “in,” “for,” or name the field.  
* Do not write “let’s delve into.” Begin the explanation.  
* Do not write “In conclusion” or “Ultimately.” End with the conclusion itself.

Use transitions only when they genuinely show a relationship. Avoid mechanical chains of “Moreover,” “Furthermore,” “Additionally,” and “Consequently.” Prefer plain links such as “But,” “So,” “For example,” “At the same time,” or no transition at all.

Do not force every paragraph into a tidy structure. Vary paragraph length according to the idea. Some points can be handled in one blunt sentence; others may need a short example or qualification. Avoid three-item lists merely because they sound balanced.

Use concrete evidence:

* Name people, places, dates, tools, decisions, examples, and constraints where relevant.  
* Explain why a point matters in this situation, rather than making a broad claim that fits any topic.  
* Include a real limitation, tradeoff, doubt, or exception when one exists.  
* Prefer an example from the actual context over a generic hypothetical.

Sound like a person with a viewpoint:

* Make a defensible judgment instead of presenting every issue as perfectly balanced.  
* Use restrained first person when appropriate: “I think,” “I found,” “In my experience,” or “My concern is.”  
* Do not manufacture personal experiences or uncertainty. Only include what is true.  
* Let minor variations in rhythm remain. Do not polish every sentence into the same length or cadence.

Keep grammar and facts accurate. “Natural” does not mean adding misspellings, bad grammar, fake anecdotes, random slang, or deliberate mistakes. It means removing generic filler, preserving genuine voice, and allowing writing to be specific rather than uniformly polished.

Before finalizing, check:

1. Does every paragraph add a new, concrete idea?  
2. Could a reader tell what I actually think or recommend?  
3. Have I replaced broad praise with evidence?  
4. Have I removed unnecessary headings, summaries, and repeated conclusions?  
5. Does this sound like the author’s normal vocabulary and level of formality?

Do not write to sound impressive. Write to be understood.

Do not use inflated, vague, or fashionable language when a plain word is stronger.

Do not use “delve,” “tapestry,” “realm,” “landscape,” “intricate,” “pivotal,” “crucial,” “multifaceted,” “transformative,” “vibrant,” “seamless,” “meticulous,” “robust,” “foster,” “leverage,” “navigate,” “underscore,” “showcase,” “unlock,” “garner,” “bolster,” or “utilize” unless the word is necessary and more exact than a simple alternative.

Do not begin with empty scene-setting such as:

* “In today’s fast-paced world…”  
* “In an ever-evolving landscape…”  
* “In the modern era…”  
* “In the realm of…”  
* “As we navigate…”  
* “It is important to note…”  
* “It is worth noting…”  
* “At its core…”  
* “When it comes to…”  
* “In recent years…”

Do not announce what the writing will do. Do not write “This article will explore,” “Let’s delve into,” “In this post, we will discuss,” or “This essay aims to highlight.” Begin with the point itself.

Do not use fake emphasis. Do not write “very,” “really,” “extremely,” “incredibly,” “highly,” “truly,” “undeniably,” or “without a doubt” unless evidence supports that intensity.

Do not write generic importance claims:

* “plays a crucial role”  
* “is essential”  
* “is a key factor”  
* “has significant implications”  
* “offers a wide range of benefits”  
* “can make a meaningful difference”  
* “has the potential to transform”  
* “is a testament to”  
* “highlights the importance of”

Instead, state what changes, for whom, under what conditions, and why.

Do not pile up transitions. Do not begin every paragraph with “Moreover,” “Furthermore,” “Additionally,” “Consequently,” “Therefore,” “In addition,” or “On the other hand.” Use a transition only if it reveals a real connection. Often, no transition is better.

Do not force a balanced argument when the evidence is not balanced. Do not use “On the one hand … on the other hand …” just to sound fair. State the strongest case, then explain the real limitation or objection.

Do not use formulaic contrast constructions repeatedly:

* “It is not just X; it is Y.”  
* “Not only X, but also Y.”  
* “Whether you are X or Y…”  
* “From X to Y…”  
* “More than just X…”  
* “X is not merely Y—it is Z.”

Use them only when the contrast is genuinely useful.

Do not make claims that could apply to any subject. If a sentence could be pasted into an article about education, marketing, health, technology, or leadership without changing it, rewrite it with specific details.

Do not replace evidence with praise. Do not call something “innovative,” “powerful,” “valuable,” “effective,” “comprehensive,” or “groundbreaking” without explaining exactly what it does and how that judgment is supported.

Do not use long lists of adjectives. Do not write “a dynamic, innovative, inclusive, sustainable, and forward-thinking approach.” Name the one or two qualities that matter, then show them.

Do not repeat the same conclusion in the introduction, body, summary, and final paragraph. Say it once well. Each paragraph must add information, evidence, interpretation, or a useful qualification.

Do not add a summary merely because the text is ending. Do not automatically write “In conclusion,” “To sum up,” “In summary,” “Ultimately,” or “The key takeaway is.” End when the final point has been made.

Do not over-explain obvious points. Do not define common terms unless the audience needs the definition. Do not turn every answer into a beginner’s guide.

Do not rely on generic examples. Do not invent a hypothetical scenario when a real example, source, detail, or concrete consequence is available.

Do not use statistics, citations, names, quotations, dates, or technical claims unless they can be verified. Do not create placeholder references or vague attributions such as “studies show” or “experts agree.”

Do not pretend certainty. Do not hide limitations, disagreement, uncertainty, tradeoffs, or missing evidence. State what is known, what is inferred, and what remains unclear.

Do not write in a uniformly polished rhythm. Do not make every sentence the same length or structure. Mix short direct sentences with longer explanatory ones when the idea requires it.

Do not make every paragraph the same size. A sharp observation may need one sentence. A difficult idea may need a fuller explanation. Let the material determine the shape.

Do not use an em dash in every paragraph. Do not use parentheses to add information that belongs in the sentence. Do not use semicolons merely to sound formal.

Do not make a list when a sentence would be clearer. Do not add headings, subheadings, “Key Takeaways,” FAQs, or bullet points unless they improve navigation for the reader.

Do not overuse rhetorical questions. Do not ask a question only to answer it immediately. State the point directly.

Do not use empty reassurance or sales language:

* “The good news is…”  
* “The possibilities are endless.”  
* “The future is bright.”  
* “This exciting opportunity…”  
* “A game-changer.”  
* “A win-win.”  
* “A new era.”  
* “The next level.”

Do not sound like a brochure, press release, consultant deck, or automated help page unless that tone is specifically required.

Do not imitate another person’s voice. Do not manufacture personal stories, emotions, doubts, mistakes, or experiences. Do not add intentional grammar errors, misspellings, slang, or random imperfections. Natural writing is not careless writing.

Do not remove every sign of personality. Keep genuine preferences, direct judgments, precise observations, and honest uncertainty when they belong.

Do not use words that the writer would never normally use. Match the author’s real vocabulary, level of formality, and sentence rhythm.

Do not submit writing that the author cannot explain, defend, revise, or trace back to their own notes and sources.

Before finalizing, remove every sentence that does not do at least one of these things: provide a fact, give an example, explain a reason, make a supported judgment, address a limitation, or move the reader to the next necessary idea.
